It’s High Time Businesses Create and Train AI Agents or Face Inevitable Collapse

Rivals already hand routine work to agents. Here is how to create, train, and govern yours before the gap widens.

SK

Shuja Khan

13 Nov 2026 · 8 min read

Businesses that do not train AI agents will lose speed, margin, and clients to rivals that do. An AI agent is software that pursues a goal, uses connected tools, and returns a result or an exception for review. GaaS, or Agentic as a Service, packages this capability as a managed service, so you can adopt agents without building everything yourself. At Miaoon, we see the pattern across agencies and brands: the teams that delegate routine work to trained agents ship faster, and the teams that wait fall behind a little more every quarter.

Summary

  1. Agents complete bounded work toward a goal, while traditional software waits for a person to operate it.
  2. GaaS lets a business adopt managed agents without building the full stack in-house.
  3. Collapse rarely arrives overnight. It arrives as slower delivery, higher cost, and lost clients.
  4. To train AI agents, you define goals, supply approved data, connect limited tools, and test against real cases.
  5. Start with one repeatable task, measure accepted outcomes, and expand only after it works.

Businesses must train AI agents now or lose ground to faster rivals

Speed decides most competitive contests, and agents change speed. A trained agent drafts the report, files the assets, chases the approval, and flags the exception while your team works on something else. A competitor with that capacity answers faster, quotes faster, and delivers faster.

Because of this, the gap compounds. One agent saves a few hours a week. Ten agents running across reporting, onboarding, and follow-ups change how many clients one team can serve. Meanwhile, a business without agents must hire to grow, and hiring costs more and moves slower.

The shift also has a market logic. Buyers want results more than tools, and services already make up over 72% of U.S. GDP. Investors back the same direction, since VC-backed companies raised more than $80 billion in the first quarter of 2025, with software leading. Agents sit exactly where these two forces meet.

GaaS makes agents a service you can adopt instead of a lab project

GaaS, or Agentic as a Service, is a service model in which managed AI agents perform bounded work for users or systems. The provider runs the models, tool connections, monitoring, and approval steps. You supply the goal, the approved data, the business rules, and the accountable reviewers.

That means a small business no longer needs a research team to start. You can create an agent around one task, connect it to the tools you already use, and review its work. Feluda’s explainer on GaaS vs SaaS describes the split clearly: SaaS provides software capabilities, while GaaS provides managed execution toward a goal.

However, adoption still needs ownership. The provider manages the runtime, but you decide what the agent may do. Therefore, treat GaaS as a service you direct, not a switch you flip.

Collapse starts as slow erosion, not a single event

The word collapse sounds dramatic, so let us be precise. Most businesses will not vanish because they skipped agents. Instead, they will lose ground in small, steady steps until recovery becomes expensive.

These are general tendencies, not guarantees. Even so, the direction is clear. Each quarter of delay lets rivals build better agents, richer data, and stronger habits.

What it means to create and train an agent

Creating an agent does not mean building a model from scratch. In most businesses, it means defining the work, the boundaries, and the checks. Training then means improving the agent through instructions, examples, testing, and feedback.

An agent needs four ingredients.

A clear goal

State the outcome in one sentence, such as “collect last week’s campaign data and draft a summary for the account manager.” A vague goal produces vague work.

Approved data

List the sources the agent may read. Include brand guidelines, past reports, and client rules. Exclude anything the agent does not need.

Limited tools

Connect only the systems the task requires, and grant the lowest permission that works. For example, let the agent read a project board before you let it write to one.

Human review rules

Decide which actions need approval and which cases the agent must escalate. As a result, people stay accountable for sensitive decisions.

How to train AI agents in seven steps

Follow this sequence to build a dependable agent.

  1. Pick one repeatable task with a result you can check.
  2. Write the goal, the steps, and the stopping conditions in plain language.
  3. Collect 10 to 20 real examples of the task, including messy ones.
  4. Run the agent on those examples and compare its output to the best human version.
  5. Note every error, then refine the instructions, data, and rules.
  6. Add human approval for any action a client or customer will see.
  7. Release the agent on live work, review exceptions daily, and keep refining.

Importantly, training never fully ends. Models change, tools change, and your business changes. Therefore, schedule a regular review of every agent you run.

Agency tasks to hand agents first

Agencies and marketing teams hold many repeatable tasks that suit agents. The best starting points share three traits: they repeat often, they cross several tools, and they have a checkable result.

Turns briefs into organised tasks

An agent reads a new client brief, extracts deliverables and deadlines, and drafts the tasks. Your producer then reviews the plan instead of typing it.

Prepares client reports

The agent gathers data from approved sources and drafts a summary with each number traced to its origin. Consequently, your account team spends its time on insight.

Chases approvals and feedback

The agent sends reminders when a client goes quiet and logs replies against the right project. Your timelines hold even during busy weeks.

Escalates creative and sensitive calls

Brand tone, concept direction, and pricing stay with people. When the agent meets an unclear case, it stops and asks.

Governance mistakes that sink agent projects

Agents fail for predictable reasons. Most failures come from weak boundaries, not weak technology.

Here are the mistakes to avoid.

  1. Giving an agent full access to every system on day one.
  2. Skipping tests on messy, real examples.
  3. Measuring agent activity instead of accepted outcomes.
  4. Leaving no named owner for exceptions.
  5. Ignoring data flow, so client information travels somewhere unapproved.
  6. Letting model or prompt updates go live without retesting.

In addition, never let an agent claim a task is complete without evidence. Require it to show its sources and steps. That habit catches false completions early.

Build, buy, or adopt GaaS

You have three routes to agents. The right choice depends on your skills, budget, and risk tolerance.

RouteBest forMain trade-off
Build in-houseTeams with strong engineering and unique needsHighest cost and longest setup
Buy a point toolOne narrow task with a ready solutionLimited flexibility across systems
Adopt GaaSTeams that want managed agents across several toolsNeeds clear governance and a trusted provider

For most agencies and small businesses, GaaS offers the fastest start. It also keeps maintenance, monitoring, and updates with the provider. Even then, you must still define goals, limits, and reviewers.

Conclusion

The case for agents is simple. They give a business more capacity without a matching rise in headcount, and they free people for the work only people can do. Rivals already use them, and the advantage grows with every month of practice.

You do not need to automate everything. Instead, choose one task, train an agent on real examples, review its work, and expand with evidence. Businesses that begin now will build skill and data that late starters cannot copy quickly. Those that wait will compete on cost and speed against opponents who learned months earlier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why should businesses train AI agents now?

Agents give early adopters more capacity, faster delivery, and lower cost per task. Because the advantage compounds with practice, late starters face a widening gap. Starting with one small task lets you learn at low risk.

What does it mean to train an AI agent?

Training an agent means improving its results through clear instructions, approved data, real examples, testing, and feedback. In most businesses, it does not involve building a new model. It involves defining the work and refining the agent until its output meets your standard.

What is GaaS?

GaaS stands for Agentic as a Service. It is a service model where managed AI agents perform bounded work toward a goal, using software and tools on your behalf. The provider runs the agent platform, while you set goals, limits, and reviews.

Will a business really collapse without AI agents?

Collapse is rarely sudden. Businesses without agents tend to lose ground through slower delivery, higher costs, and lost clients. The risk is greatest in industries where speed and margin decide who wins work.

How long does it take to train a useful agent?

A narrow, well-defined task can reach a useful standard in a few weeks of testing and refinement. Broader tasks take longer. Either way, plan for ongoing review after launch.

Which tasks should we give an agent first?

Choose repeatable, multi-step work with a checkable result. Client reporting, brief intake, and approval follow-ups are strong choices. Keep brand judgment and pricing with people.

Is it safe to let agents use client data?

It can be safe with strict limits. Restrict each agent to the data and tools its task needs, record its actions, and require approval for anything client-facing. Also confirm where your provider sends and stores data.

Do we need engineers to use GaaS?

Not always. Managed services let non-technical teams define goals and review output. However, someone must own governance, so name a responsible person before launch.
